#### TEM vs SEM

↑ **Parent:** [Electron microscope](#electron-microscope)

[https://www.thermofisher.com/uk/en/home/materials-science/learning-center/applications/sem-tem-difference.html#:~:text=The%20difference%20between%20SEM%20and,sample)%20to%20create%20an%20image.](https://www.thermofisher.com/uk/en/home/materials-science/learning-center/applications/sem-tem-difference.html#:~:text=The%20difference%20between%20SEM%20and,sample)%20to%20create%20an%20image.)

[SEM](#scanning-electron-microscope): sample does not need to be ultra thin, you get a 3D image. Lower resolution possible.

#### Cryogenic electron microscopy

↑ **Parent:** [Electron microscope](#electron-microscope)  
🏷️ **Tags:** [2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry](nobel-prize.md#2017-nobel-prize-in-chemistry)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryogenic_electron_microscopy)

This technique has managed to determine protein 3D structures for proteins that people were not able to crystallize for [X-ray crystallography](#x-ray-crystallography).

It is said however that cryoEM is even fiddlier than [X-ray crystallography](#x-ray-crystallography), so it is mostly attempted if crystallization attempts fail.

By looking at [Figure 1. "A cryoEM image"](#image-a-cryoem-image), you can easily understand the basics of cryoEM.

We just put a gazillion copies of our molecule of interest in a solution, and then image all of them in the frozen water.

Each one of them appears in the image in a random rotated view, so given enough of those point of view images, we can deduce the entire 3D structure of the molecule.

[Ciro Santilli](ciro-santilli.md) once watched a talk by [Richard Henderson](nobel-prize.md#richard-henderson-biologist) about cryoEM circa 2020, where he mentioned that he witnessed some students in the 1980's going to Germany, and coming into contact with early cryoEM. And when they came back, they just told their [principal investigator](education.md#principal-investigator): "I'm going to drop my PhD theme and focus exclusively on cryoEM". That's how hot the cryo thing was! So cool.

#### Super-resolution microscopy

↑ **Parent:** [Fluorescence microscope](#fluorescence-microscope)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super-resolution_microscopy)

Super-resolution means resolution beyond the [diffraction limit](#diffraction-limit).

First you shine a lot of light which saturates most [fluorophores](condensed-matter-physics.md#fluorescence), leaving very few active.

They you can observe [fluorophores](condensed-matter-physics.md#fluorescence) firing one by one. Their exact position is a bit stochastic and beyond the [diffraction limit](#diffraction-limit), but so long as there aren't to many in close proximity, you can wait for it to fire a bunch of times, and the center of the [Gaussian](formalization-of-mathematics.md#gaussian-function) is the actual location.

From this we see that super-resolution microscopy is basically a space-time tradeoff: the more time we wait, the better spacial resolution we get. But we can't do it if things are moving too fast in the sample.

Tradeoff with [cryoEM](#cryogenic-electron-microscopy): you get to see things moving in live cell. [Electron microscopy](#electron-microscope) fully kills cells, so you have no chance of seeing anything that moves ever.

Caveats:
- initial illumination to saturate most fluorophores I think can still kill cells, things get harder the less light you put in. So it's not like you don't kill things at all necessarily, you just get a chance not to
- the presence fluorophore disturbs the system slightly, and is not at the same Exact location of the protein of interest

##### STED microscopy

↑ **Parent:** [Super-resolution microscopy](#super-resolution-microscopy)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STED_microscopy)

[Stefan Hell](nobel-prize.md#stefan-hell) was really excited by this as of 2023.

Instead of shining a light over the entire sample to saturate it, you illuminate just a small bit instead.

He was basically saying that this truly brings the resolution to the actual physical limits, going much much beyond 2014 Nobel prize levels.

### X-ray crystallography

↑ **Parent:** [Type of microscopy](#type-of-microscopy)  
🏷️ **Tags:** [Crystallography](condensed-matter-physics.md#crystallography)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography)

One of its main applications is to determine the 3D structure of [proteins](protein.md).

Sometimes you are not able to crystallize the proteins however, and the method cannot be used.

Crystallizing is not simple because:
- you need a considerable amount of the protein
- sometimes it only crystallizes if you add some extra small chemical that stabilizes it

[Cryogenic electron microscopy](#cryogenic-electron-microscopy) can sometimes determine the structures of proteins that failed crystallization.

#### Lab vs cyclotron X-ray crystallography

↑ **Parent:** [X-ray crystallography](#x-ray-crystallography)

[cyclotrons](particle-physics.md#cyclotron) produce the better images, but they are expensive/you have to move to them and order a timeslot.

Lab-based just use some X-ray source from the lab, so it is much move convenient e.g. for a [pharmaceutical company](biology.md#pharmaceutical-company) doing a bunch of images. The Wikipedia image shows such a self-contained lab system: [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Freezed_XRD.jpg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Freezed_XRD.jpg)

#### History of X-ray crystallography

↑ **Parent:** [X-ray crystallography](#x-ray-crystallography)

- 1958: [myoglobin structure resolution (1958)](protein.md#myoglobin-structure-resolution-1958). The first [protein](protein.md) to be resolved.
- 1965: [lysozyme structure resolution (1965)](protein.md#lysozyme-structure-resolution-1965). The second [protein](protein.md) to be resolved.
