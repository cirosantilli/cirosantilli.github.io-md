# Particle physics

↑ **Parent:** [Physics](physics.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics)

Currently an informal name for the [Standard Model](standard-model.md)

Chronological outline of the key theories:
- [Maxwell's equations](electromagnetism.md#maxwell-s-equations)
- [Schrödinger equation](quantum-mechanics.md#schrodinger-equation)
  - Date: 1926
  - Numerical predictions:
    - [hydrogen spectral line](quantum-mechanics.md#hydrogen-emission-spectrum), excluding finer structure such as 2p up and down split: [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine-structure_constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine-structure_constant)
- [Dirac equation](relativistic-quantum-mechanics.md#dirac-equation)
  - Date: 1928
  - Numerical predictions:
    - [hydrogen spectral line](quantum-mechanics.md#hydrogen-emission-spectrum) including 2p split, but excluding even finer structure such as [Lamb shift](quantum-field-theory.md#lamb-shift)
  - Qualitative predictions:
    - Antimatter
    - Spin as part of the equation
- [quantum electrodynamics](quantum-field-theory.md#quantum-electrodynamics)
  - Date: 1947 onwards
  - Numerical predictions:
    - [Lamb shift](quantum-field-theory.md#lamb-shift)
  - Qualitative predictions:
    - Antimatter
    - [spin](relativistic-quantum-mechanics.md#spin-physics) as part of the equation

#### Radioactive decay

↑ **Parent:** [Nuclear reaction](#nuclear-reaction)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay)

[Ciro Santilli](ciro-santilli.md) finds it interesting that radioactive decay basically kickstarted the domain of [nuclear physics](#nuclear-physics) by essentially providing a natural [particle accelerator](#particle-accelerator) from a chunk of radioactive element.

The discovery process was particularly interesting, including [Henri Becquerel](physicist.md#henri-becquerel)'s luck while observing [phosphorescence](condensed-matter-physics.md#phosphorescence), and [Marie Curie](physicist.md#marie-curie)'s observation that the [uranium](chemistry.md#uranium) [ore](astronomy.md#ore) were more radioactive than pure uranium, and must therefore contain other even more radioactive substances, which lead to the discovery of [polonium](chemistry.md#polonium) ([half-life](#half-life) 138 days) and [radium](chemistry.md#radium) (half-life 1600 years).

##### Type of radioactive decay

↑ **Parent:** [Radioactive decay](#radioactive-decay)

###### Alpha decay

↑ **Parent:** [Type of radioactive decay](#type-of-radioactive-decay)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_decay)

Most of the [helium](chemistry.md#helium) in the [Earth](astronomy.md#earth)'s atmosphere comes from alpha decay, since helium is lighter than air and naturally escapes out out of the atmosphere.

Wiki mentions that alpha decay is well modelled as a [quantum tunnelling](quantum-mechanics.md#quantum-tunnelling) event, see also [Geiger-Nuttall law](#geiger-nuttall-law).

As a result of that law, alpha particles have relatively little energy variation around 5 MeV or a speed of about 5% of the [speed of light](photon.md#speed-of-light) for any element, because the energy is inversely exponentially proportional to [half-life](#half-life). This is because:
- if the energy is much larger, decay is very fast and we don't have time to study the [isotope](#isotope)
- if the energy is much smaller, decay is very rare and we don't have enough events to observe at all

###### Gamma ray

↑ **Parent:** [Type of radioactive decay](#type-of-radioactive-decay)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray)

Most commonly known as a byproduct [radioactive decay](#radioactive-decay).

Their energy is very high compared example to more common radiation such as [visible spectrum](photon.md#visible-spectrum), and there is a neat reason for that: it's because the [strong force](quantum-field-theory.md#strong-interaction) that binds [nuclei](#atomic-nucleus) is strong so transitions lead to large energy changes.

A [decay scheme](#decay-scheme) such as [Figure "caesium-137 decay scheme"](chemistry.md#image-caesium-137-decay-scheme) illustrates well how gamma radiation happens as a byproduct of [radioactive decay](#radioactive-decay) due to the existence of [nuclear isomer](#nuclear-isomer).

Gamma rays are pretty cool as they give us insight into the energy levels/different configurations of the nucleus.

They have also been used as early sources of high energy particles for [particle physics](particle-physics.md) experiments before the development of [particle accelerators](#particle-accelerator), serving a similar purpose to [cosmic rays](astronomy.md#cosmic-ray) in those early days.

But [gamma rays](#gamma-ray) they were more convenient in some cases because you could more easily manage them inside a [laboratory](science.md#laboratory) rather than have to go climb some bloody mountain or a [balloon](technology.md#balloon).

The [positron](standard-model.md#positron) for example was first observed on [cosmic rays](astronomy.md#cosmic-ray), but better confirmed in [gamma ray](#gamma-ray) experiments by [Carl David Anderson](physicist.md#carl-david-anderson).
