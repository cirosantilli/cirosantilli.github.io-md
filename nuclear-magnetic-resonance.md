# Nuclear magnetic resonance

↑ **Parent:** [Nuclear magnetic moment](nuclear-magnetic-moment.md)  
🏷️ **Tags:** [Tomography](tomography.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance)

[Ciro Santilli](ciro-santilli-split.md) once visited the chemistry department of a world leading university, and the chemists there were obsessed with [NMR](nuclear-magnetic-resonance.md). They had small benchtop [NMR](nuclear-magnetic-resonance.md) machines. They had larger machines. They had a room full of huge machines. They had them in corridors and on desk tops. Chemists really love that stuff. More precisely, these are used for [NMR spectroscopy](nuclear-magnetic-resonance-spectroscopy.md), which helps identify what a sample is made of.

Basically measures the concentration of certain isotopes in a region of space.

<a id="video-introduction-to-nmr-by-allery-chemistry"></a>
**[Video 4](#video-introduction-to-nmr-by-allery-chemistry). Introduction to NMR by Allery Chemistry.** [Source](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e-vSNPW1NO0). - only works with an odd number of [nucleons](nucleon.md)
- apply strong [magnetic field](magnetic-field.md), this separates the energy of up and down spins. Most spins align with field.
- send [radio waves](radio-wave.md) into sample to make nucleons go to upper energy level. We can see that the energy difference is small since we are talking about radio waves, low frequency.
- when nucleon goes back down, it re-emits radio waves, and we detect that. TODO: how do we not get that confused with the input wave, which is presumably at the same frequency? It appears to send pulses, and then wait for the response.
