#### The direct product of two cyclic groups of coprime order is another cyclic group

↑ **Parent:** [Important discrete mathematical group](#important-discrete-mathematical-group)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_direct_product_of_two_cyclic_groups_of_coprime_order_is_another_cyclic_group)

You just map the value (1, 1) $C_m \times C_n$ to the value 1 of $C_{mn}$, and it works out. E.g. for $C_2 \times C_3$, the [group generated by](#generating-set-of-a-group) of (1, 1) is:
```
0 = (0, 0)
1 = (1, 1)
2 = (0, 2)
3 = (1, 0)
4 = (0, 1)
5 = (1, 2)
6 = (0, 0) = 0
```

###### Stabilizer (group)

↑ **Parent:** [Permutation group](#permutation-group)

Suppose we have a given [permutation group](#permutation-group) that acts on a set of n elements.

If we pick k elements of the set, the stabilizer subgroup of those k elements is a subgroup of the given permutation group that keeps those elements unchanged.

Note that an analogous definition can be given for non-finite groups. Also note that the case for all finite groups is covered by the permutation definition since [all groups are isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group](#all-groups-are-isomorphic-to-a-subgroup-of-the-symmetric-group)

###### Mathieu group

↑ **Parent:** [Sporadic group](#sporadic-group)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathieu_group)

Contains the first [sporadic groups](#sporadic-group) discovered by far: 11 and 12 in 1861, and 22, 23 and 24 in 1973. And therefore presumably the simplest! The next sporadic ones discovered were the [Janko groups](#janko-group), only in 1965!

Each [$M_n$](linear-algebra.md#matrix-ring) is a [permutation group](#permutation-group) on $n$ elements. There isn't an obvious algorithmic relationship between $n$ and the actual group.

Their; [k-transitive group](#k-transitive-group) properties seem to be the main characterization, according to Wikipedia:
- 22 is 3-transitive but not 4-transitive.
- four of them (11, 12, 23 and 24) are the only [sporadic](#sporadic-group) [4-transitive](#k-transitive-group) groups as per the [classification of 4-transitive groups](#classification-of-4-transitive-groups) (no known simpler proof as of 2021), which sounds like a reasonable characterization. Note that 12 and 25 are also 5 transitive.
Looking at the [classification of k-transitive groups](#classification-of-k-transitive-groups) we see that the Mathieu groups are the only families of 4 and 5 transitive groups other than [symmetric groups](#symmetric-group) and [alternating groups](#alternating-group). 3-transitive is not as nice, so let's just say it is the [stabilizer](#stabilizer-group) of $M_{23}$ and be done with it.

### Isomorphism

↑ **Parent:** [Group isomorphism](#group-isomorphism)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism)

Something analogous to a [group isomorphism](#group-isomorphism), but that preserves whatever properties the given algebraic object has. E.g. for a [field](#field-mathematics), we also have to preserve multiplication in addition to addition.

Other common examples include isomorphisms of [vector spaces](linear-algebra.md#vector-space) and [field](#field-mathematics). But since both of those two are much simpler than groups in [classification](mathematics.md#classification-mathematics), as they are both determined by number of elements/dimension alone, see:
- [classification of finite fields](#classification-of-finite-fields)
- [all vector spaces of the same dimension on a given field are isomorphic](linear-algebra.md#classification-of-vector-spaces)
we tend to not talk about isomorphisms so much in those contexts.

### Group homomorphism

↑ **Parent:** [Group isomorphism](#group-isomorphism)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_homomorphism)

Like isomorphism, but does not have to be one-to-one: multiple different inputs can have the same output.

The image is as for any function smaller or equal in size as the domain of course.

This brings us to the key intuition about group homomorphisms: they are a way to split out a larger group into smaller groups that retains a subset of the original structure.

As shown by the [fundamental theorem on homomorphisms](#fundamental-theorem-on-homomorphisms), each group homomorphism is fully characterized by a [normal subgroup](#normal-subgroup) of the domain.

### Semidirect product

↑ **Parent:** [Direct product of groups](#direct-product-of-groups)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semidirect_product)

As per [https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Semidirect_product&oldid=1040813965#Properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Semidirect_product&oldid=1040813965#Properties), unlike the [Direct product](formalization-of-mathematics.md#direct-product), the semidirect product of two goups is neither [unique](formalization-of-mathematics.md#uniqueness), nor does it always [exist](formalization-of-mathematics.md#existence), and there is no known algorithmic way way to tell if one exists or not.

This is because reaching the "output" of the semidirect produt of two groups requires extra non-obvious information that might not exist. This is because the semi-direct product is based on the [product of group subsets](#product-of-group-subsets). So you start with two small and completely independent groups, and it is not obvious how to join them up, i.e. how to define the group operation of the product group that is compatible with that of the two smaller input groups. Contrast this with the [Direct product](formalization-of-mathematics.md#direct-product), where the composition is simple: just use the group operation of each group on either side.

Product of group subsets

So in other words, it is not a [function](formalization-of-mathematics.md#function-mathematics) like the [Direct product](formalization-of-mathematics.md#direct-product). The semidiret product is therefore more like a property of three groups. 

The semidirect product is more general than the [direct product of groups](#direct-product-of-groups) when thinking about the [group extension problem](#group-extension-problem), because with the [direct product of groups](#direct-product-of-groups), both subgroups of the larger group are necessarily also normal (trivial projection [group homomorphism](#group-homomorphism) on either side), while for the semidirect product, only one of them does.

Conversely, [https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Semidirect_product&oldid=1040813965](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Semidirect_product&oldid=1040813965) explains that if $G = N \rtimes H$, and besides the implied requirement that N is normal, H is also normal, then $G = N \times H$.

Smallest example: $D_6 = C_3 \rtimes C_2$ where $D$ is a [dihedral group](#dihedral-group) and $C$ are [cyclic groups](#cyclic-group). $C_3$ (the rotation) is a normal subgroup of $D_6$, but $C_2$ (the flip) is not.

Note that with the [Direct product](formalization-of-mathematics.md#direct-product) instead we get $C_6$ and not $D_6$, i.e. $C_3 \times C_2 = C_6$ as per [the direct product of two cyclic groups of coprime order is another cyclic group](#the-direct-product-of-two-cyclic-groups-of-coprime-order-is-another-cyclic-group).

#### Relationship between the quotient group and direct products

↑ **Parent:** [Quotient group](#quotient-group)

Although quotients look a bit real number division, there are some important differences with the "group analog of multiplication" of [direct product of groups](#direct-product-of-groups).

If a group is isomorphic to the [direct product of groups](#direct-product-of-groups), we can take a quotient of the product to retrieve one of the groups, which is somewhat analogous to division: [https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/723707/how-is-the-quotient-group-related-to-the-direct-product-group](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/723707/how-is-the-quotient-group-related-to-the-direct-product-group)

The "converse" is not always true however: a group does not need to be isomorphic to the product of one of its [normal subgroups](#normal-subgroup) and the associated [quotient group](#quotient-group). The wiki page provides an example:

> Given G and a normal subgroup N, then G is a group extension of G/N by N. One could ask whether this extension is trivial or split; in other words, one could ask whether G is a direct product or semidirect product of N and G/N. This is a special case of the extension problem. An example where the extension is not split is as follows: Let $G = Z4 = {0, 1, 2, 3}$, and $ = {0, 2}$ which is isomorphic to Z2. Then G/N is also isomorphic to Z2. But Z2 has only the trivial automorphism, so the only semi-direct product of N and G/N is the direct product. Since Z4 is different from Z2 × Z2, we conclude that G is not a semi-direct product of N and G/N.

#### Normal subgroup

↑ **Parent:** [Quotient group](#quotient-group)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_subgroup)

Ultimate explanation: [https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/776039/intuition-behind-normal-subgroups/3732426#3732426](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/776039/intuition-behind-normal-subgroups/3732426#3732426)

Only normal subgroups can be used to form [quotient groups](#quotient-group): their key definition is that they plus their cosets form a group.

Intuition:
- [https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/776039/intuition-behind-normal-subgroups](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/776039/intuition-behind-normal-subgroups)
- [https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/1014535/is-there-any-intuitive-understanding-of-normal-subgroup/1014791](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/1014535/is-there-any-intuitive-understanding-of-normal-subgroup/1014791)

One key intuition is that "a normal subgroup is the [kernel](#kernel-algebra)" of a [group homomorphism](#group-homomorphism), and the normal subgroup plus cosets are isomorphic to the image of the isomorphism, which is what the [fundamental theorem on homomorphisms](#fundamental-theorem-on-homomorphisms) says.

Therefore "there aren't that many [group homomorphism](#group-homomorphism)", and a normal subgroup it is a concrete and natural way to uniquely represent that homomorphism.

The best way to think about the, is to always think first: what is the homomorphism? And then work out everything else from there.

##### Simple group

↑ **Parent:** [Normal subgroup](#normal-subgroup)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_group)

Does not have any non-trivial [normal subgroup](#normal-subgroup).

And therefore, going back to our intuition that due to the [fundamental theorem on homomorphisms](#fundamental-theorem-on-homomorphisms) there is one normal group per homomorphism, a simple group is one that has no non-trivial homomorphisms.

## Ring (mathematics)

↑ **Parent:** [Group](group.md)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_(mathematics))

A [Ring](#ring-mathematics) can be seen as a generalization of a [field](#field-mathematics) where:
- multiplication is not necessarily [commutative](#commutative-property). If this is satisfied, we can call it a [commutative ring](#commutative-ring).
- multiplication may not have [inverse elements](algebra.md#inverse-element). If this is satisfied, we can call it a [division ring](#division-ring).

Addition however has to be [commutative](#commutative-property) and have inverses, i.e. it is an [Abelian group](#abelian-group).

The simplest example of a ring which is not a full fledged [field](#field-mathematics) and with [commutative](#commutative-property) multiplication are the [integers](formalization-of-mathematics.md#integer). Notably, no inverses exist except for the identity itself and -1. E.g. the inverse of 2 would be 1/2 which is not in the [set](formalization-of-mathematics.md#set-mathematics). More specifically, the [integers](formalization-of-mathematics.md#integer) are a [commutative ring](#commutative-ring).

A [polynomial ring](formalization-of-mathematics.md#polynomial-ring) is another example with the same properties as the [integers](formalization-of-mathematics.md#integer).

The simplest [non-commutative](#commutative-ring), [non-division](#division-ring) is is the set of all 2x2 [matrices](linear-algebra.md#matrix) of [real numbers](formalization-of-mathematics.md#real-number):
- we know that 2x2 matrix multiplication is non-commutative in general
- some 2x2 matrices have a multiplicative inverse, but others don't
Note that [$GL(n)$](geometry.md#general-linear-group) is not a ring because you can by addition reach the zero matrix.

### Field (mathematics)

↑ **Parent:** [Ring (mathematics)](#ring-mathematics)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(mathematics))

A [ring](#ring-mathematics) where multiplication is [commutative](#commutative-property) and there is always an inverse.

A field can be seen as an [Abelian group](#abelian-group) that has two [group operations](#group-operation) defined on it: addition and multiplication.

And then, besides each of the two operations obeying the [group axioms](#group-axiom) individually, and they are compatible between themselves according to the [distributive property](#distributive-property).

Basically the nicest, least restrictive, 2-operation type of [algebra](algebra.md).

Examples:
- [real numbers](formalization-of-mathematics.md#real-number)
- [rational numbers](formalization-of-mathematics.md#rational-number)

#### Finite field

↑ **Parent:** [Field (mathematics)](#field-mathematics)  
🏷️ **Tags:** [Finite algebraic structure](algebra.md#finite-algebraic-structure)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_field)

A convenient notation for the elements of $GF(n)$ of prime order is to use [integers](formalization-of-mathematics.md#integer), e.g. for $GF(7)$ we could write:

$$
GR(7) = \{-3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3\}
$$

which makes it clear what is the additive inverse of each element, although sometimes a notation starting from 0 is also used:

$$
GR(7) = \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}
$$

For fields of [prime](mathematics.md#prime-number) order, regular [modular arithmetic](mathematics.md#modular-arithmetic) works as the field operation.

For non-prime order, we see that [modular arithmetic](mathematics.md#modular-arithmetic) does not work because the divisors have no inverse. E.g. at order 6, 2 and 3 have no inverse, e.g. for 2:

$$
0 \times 2 = 0
1 \times 2 = 2
2 \times 2 = 4
3 \times 2 = 0
4 \times 2 = 2
5 \times 2 = 4
$$

we see that things wrap around perfecly, and 1 is never reached.

For non-prime [prime power](mathematics.md#prime-power) orders however, we can find a way, see [finite field of non-prime order](#finite-field-of-non-prime-order).

<a id="video-finite-fields-made-easy-by-randell-heyman-2015"></a>
**[Video 4](#video-finite-fields-made-easy-by-randell-heyman-2015). Finite fields made easy by Randell Heyman (2015)** [Source](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z9bTzjy4SCg). Good introduction with examples

##### Classification of finite fields

↑ **Parent:** [Finite field](#finite-field)  
🏷️ **Tags:** [Classification (mathematics)](mathematics.md#classification-mathematics)

There's exactly one field per [prime power](mathematics.md#prime-power), so all we need to specify a field is give its order, notated e.g. as $GF(n)$.

Every element of a finite field satisfies $x^{order} = x$.

It is interesting to compare this result philosophically with the [classification of finite groups](#classification-of-finite-groups): fields are more constrained as they have to have two operations, and this leads to a much simpler classification!

##### Finite field of non-prime order

↑ **Parent:** [Finite field](#finite-field)

As per [classification of finite fields](#classification-of-finite-fields) those must be of [prime power](mathematics.md#prime-power) order.

[Video 4. "Finite fields made easy by Randell Heyman (2015)"](#video-finite-fields-made-easy-by-randell-heyman-2015) at [https://youtu.be/z9bTzjy4SCg?t=159](https://youtu.be/z9bTzjy4SCg?t=159) shows how for order $9 = 3 \times 3$. Basically, for order $p^n$, we take:
- each element is a polynomial in $GF(p)[x]$, $GF(p)[x]$, the [polynomial ring over the finite field $GF(p)$](formalization-of-mathematics.md#polynomial-over-a-field) with degree smaller than $n$. We've just seen how to construct $GF(p)$ for prime $p$ above, so we're good there.
- addition works element-wise modulo on $GF(p)$
- multiplication is done modulo an [irreducible polynomial](formalization-of-mathematics.md#irreducible-polynomial) of order $n$
For a worked out example, see: [GF(4)](#gf-4).

<h5 id="gf-2">GF(2)</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Finite field](#finite-field)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GF(2))

<h5 id="gf-4">GF(4)</h5>

↑ **Parent:** [Finite field](#finite-field)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GF(4))

[Ciro Santilli](ciro-santilli.md) tried to [add this example to Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Finite_field&type=revision&diff=1044934168&oldid=1044905041), but it was reverted, so here we are, see also: [Section "Deletionism on Wikipedia"](website.md#deletionism-on-wikipedia).

This is a good first example of a field of a [finite field of non-prime order](#finite-field-of-non-prime-order), this one is a [prime power](mathematics.md#prime-power) order instead.

$4 = 2^2$, so one way to represent the elements of the field will be the to use the 4 polynomials of degree 1 over [GF(2)](#gf-2):
- 0X + 0
- 0X + 1
- 1X + 0
- 1X + 1

Note that we refer in this definition to anther field, but that is fine, because we only refer to fields of [prime](mathematics.md#prime-number) order such as [GF(2)](#gf-2), because we are dealing with [prime powers](mathematics.md#prime-power) only. And we have already defined fields of prime order easily previously with [modular arithmetic](mathematics.md#modular-arithmetic).

Over GF(2), there is only one [irreducible polynomial](formalization-of-mathematics.md#irreducible-polynomial) of degree 2:

$$
X^2+X+1
$$

Addition is defined element-wise with [modular arithmetic](mathematics.md#modular-arithmetic) modulo 2 as defined over GF(2), e.g.:

$$
(1X + 0) + (1X + 1) = (1 + 1)X + (0 + 1) = 0X + 1
$$

Multiplication is done modulo $X^2+X+1$, which ensures that the result is also of degree 1.

For example first we do a regular multiplication:

$$
(1X + 0) \times (1X + 1) = (1 \times 1)X^2 + (1 \times 1)X + (0 \times 1)X + (0 \times 1) = 1X^2 + 1X + 0
$$

Without modulo, that would not be one of the elements of the field anymore due to the $1X^2$!

So we take the modulo, we note that:

$$
1X^2 + 1X + 0 = 1(X^2+X+1) + (0X + 1)
$$

and by the definition of modulo:

$$
(1X^2 + 1X + 0) \mod (X^2+X+1) = (0X + 1)
$$

which is the final result of the multiplication.
