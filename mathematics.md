## The beauty of mathematics

↑ **Parent:** [Mathematics](mathematics.md)

[Ciro Santilli](ciro-santilli.md) intends to move his beauty list here little by little: [https://github.com/cirosantilli/mathematics/blob/master/beauty.md](https://github.com/cirosantilli/mathematics/blob/master/beauty.md)

The most beautiful things in mathematics are results that are:
- [simple to state but hard to prove](#simple-to-state-but-hard-to-prove):
  - Fermat's Last Theorem
  - [number of unknown rationality](formalization-of-mathematics.md#number-of-unknown-rationality), e.g. is $e + \pi$ [rational](formalization-of-mathematics.md#rational-number)?
  - [transcendental number conjectures](formalization-of-mathematics.md#transcendental-number-conjecture), e.g. is $e + \pi$ transcendental?
  - basically any conjecture involving [prime numbers](#prime-number):
    - [twin prime conjecture](#twin-prime-conjecture)
  - many combinatorial game questions, e.g.:
    - [first-move advantage in chess](art.md#first-move-advantage-in-chess)
- surprising results: we had intuitive reasons to believe something as possible or not, but a theorem shatters that conviction and brings us on our knees, sometimes via [pathological](#pathological-mathematics) counter-examples. General surprise themes include:
  - [classification](#classification-mathematics) of potentially [infinite](calculus.md#infinity) sets like: compact [manifolds](calculus.md#manifold), etc.
    - [classification of finite simple groups](group.md#classification-of-finite-simple-groups)
    - [classification of regular polytopes](geometry.md#classification-of-regular-polytopes)
    - [classification of closed surfaces](calculus.md#classification-of-closed-surfaces), and more [generalized Poincaré conjectures](calculus.md#generalized-poincare-conjecture)
    - classification of [wallpaper groups](group.md#wallpaper-group) and [space groups](group.md#space-group)
  - problems that are more complicated in low dimensions than high like:
    - [generalized Poincaré conjectures](calculus.md#generalized-poincare-conjecture). It is also fun to see how in many cases complexity peaks out at 4 dimensions.
    - [classification of regular polytopes](geometry.md#classification-of-regular-polytopes)
  - unpredictable magic constants:
    - why is the lowest dimension for an [exotic sphere](calculus.md#exotic-sphere) 7?
    - why is 4 the largest degree of an equation with explicit solution? [Abel-Ruffini theorem](formalization-of-mathematics.md#abel-ruffini-theorem)
  - [undecidable problems](computer-science.md#undecidable-problem), especially simple to state ones:
    - [mortal matrix problem](computer-science.md#mortal-matrix-problem)
    - sharp frontiers between solvable and unsolvable are also cool:
      - attempts at determining [specific values of the Busy beaver function](computer-science.md#specific-values-of-the-busy-beaver-function) for [Turing machines](computer-science.md#turing-machine) with a given number of states and symbols
      - related to [Diophantine equations](formalization-of-mathematics.md#diophantine-equation):
        - [decidability of Hilbert's tenth problem of a given degree and number of variables](formalization-of-mathematics.md#decidability-of-hilbert-s-tenth-problem-of-a-given-degree-and-number-of-variables)
        - [Hilbert's tenth problem over other rings](formalization-of-mathematics.md#hilbert-s-tenth-problem-over-other-rings)

  Lists:
  - [https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/139699/what-are-some-examples-of-a-mathematical-result-being-counterintuitive](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/139699/what-are-some-examples-of-a-mathematical-result-being-counterintuitive)
  - [https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/2040811/what-are-some-counter-intuitive-results-in-mathematics-that-involve-only-finite/2055458#2055458](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/2040811/what-are-some-counter-intuitive-results-in-mathematics-that-involve-only-finite/2055458#2055458)
- applications: make life easier and/or modeling some phenomena well, e.g. in [physics](physics.md). See also: [explain how to make money with the lesson](how-to-teach.md#explain-how-to-make-money-with-the-lesson)

Good lists of such problems [Lists of mathematical problems](#lists-of-mathematical-problems).

Whenever [Ciro Santilli](ciro-santilli.md) learns a bit of [mathematics](mathematics.md), he always wonders to himself:

> Am I achieving insight, or am I just memorizing definitions?

Unfortunately, due to how man books are written, it is not really possible to reach insight without first doing a bit of memorization. The better the book, the more insight is spread out, and less you have to learn before reaching each insight.

### Simple to state but hard to prove

↑ **Parent:** [The beauty of mathematics](#the-beauty-of-mathematics)

One of the most beautiful things in mathematics are theorems of conjectures that are very simple to state and understand (e.g. for [K-12](education.md#k-12), lower [undergrad](university.md#undergraduate-education) levels), but extremely hard to prove.

###### Computational complexity of modular exponentiation

↑ **Parent:** [Modular exponentiation](#modular-exponentiation)

[https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/2382011/computational-complexity-of-modular-exponentiation-from-rosens-discrete-mathem](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/2382011/computational-complexity-of-modular-exponentiation-from-rosens-discrete-mathem) mentions:

$$
b^n mod m
$$

can be calculated in:

$$
log(m)^2 log(n)
$$

Remember that $log(m)$ and $log(n)$ are the lengths in bits of $m$ and $n$, so in terms of the length in bits $M$ and $N$ we'd get:

$$
M^2 N

###### Prime k-tuple conjecture

↑ **Parent:** [Admissible prime k-tuple](#admissible-prime-k-tuple)

There are infinitely many [prime k-tuples](#prime-k-tuple) for every admissible tuple.

Generalization of the [Twin prime conjecture](#twin-prime-conjecture).

As of 2023, there was no specific admissible tuple for which it had been proven that there infinite of, only bounds of type:

> there are infinitely 2-tuple instances with at most a finite bound

But these do not specify which specific tuple, e.g. [Yitang Zhang's theorem](#yitang-zhang-s-theorem).

<h6 id="yitang-zhang-s-theorem">Yitang Zhang's theorem</h6>

↑ **Parent:** [Prime k-tuple conjecture](#prime-k-tuple-conjecture)

There are infinitely many primes with a neighbor not further apart than 70 million. This was the first such finite bound to be proven, and therefore a major breakthrough.

This implies that for at least one value (or more) below 70 million there are infinitely many repetitions, but we don't know which e.g. we could have infinitely many:

$$
k, k + 2
$$

or infinitely many:

$$
k, k + 4
$$

or infinitely many:

$$
k, k + 6,999,999
$$

or infinitely many:

$$
k, k + 2 and k, k + 4
$$

but we don't know which of those.

The [Prime k-tuple conjecture](#prime-k-tuple-conjecture) conjectures that it is all of them.

Also, if 70 million could be reduced down to 2, we would have a proof of the [Twin prime conjecture](#twin-prime-conjecture), but this method would only work for (k, k + 2).

##### Greatest common divisor

↑ **Parent:** [Prime number](#prime-number)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_common_divisor)

The "greatest common divisor" of two [integers](formalization-of-mathematics.md#integer) $x$ and $y$, denoted $\gdc(x, y)$ is the largest [natural number](formalization-of-mathematics.md#natural-number) that divides both of the integers.

For example, $\gdc(8, 12)$ is 4, because:
- 4 divides both 8 and 12
- and this is not the case for any number larger than 4. E.g.:
  - 5 divides neither one
  - 6 divides 12
  - 7 divides neither
  - 8 divides only 8

  and so on.

The [ASCII art](art.md#ascii-art) visualizations from [https://stackoverflow.com/questions/192220/what-is-the-most-efficient-elegant-way-to-parse-a-flat-table-into-a-tree/194031#194031](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/192220/what-is-the-most-efficient-elegant-way-to-parse-a-flat-table-into-a-tree/194031#194031) are worth reproducing.

- Root 1
  - Child 1.1
    - Child 1.1.1
    - Child 1.1.2
  - Child 1.2
    - Child 1.2.1
    - Child 1.2.2

As the sets:
```

|  Root 1                                                                  |
|   ________________________________    ________________________________   |
|  |  Child 1.1                     |  |  Child 1.2                     |  |
|  |   ___________    ___________   |  |   ___________    ___________   |  |
|  |  |  C 1.1.1  |  |  C 1.1.2  |  |  |  |  C 1.2.1  |  |  C 1.2.2  |  |  |
1  2  3___________4  5___________6  7  8  9___________10 11__________12 13 14
|  |________________________________|  |________________________________|  |
|__________________________________________________________________________|
```

Consider the following nested set:
```

  1, 7, mathematics
    2, 3, geometry
      3, 6, calculus
        4, 5, derivative
        5, 6, integral
      6, 7, algebra
  7, 8, physics
```

so we have a method:

###### In-order depth-first search

↑ **Parent:** [Depth-first search](#depth-first-search)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In-order_depth-first_search)

This is the order in which a [binary search tree](computer-science.md#binary-search-tree) should be traversed for ordered output, i.e.:
- everything to the left is smaller than parent
- everything to the right is larger than parent

This ordering makes sense for [binary trees](#binary-tree) and not [k-ary trees](#k-ary-tree) in general because if there are more than two nodes it is not clear what the top node should go in the middle of.

This is unlike [pre-order depth-first search](#pre-order-depth-first-search) and [post-order depth-first search](#post-order-depth-first-search) which generalize obviously to general trees.

###### Iterative in-order

↑ **Parent:** [In-order depth-first search](#in-order-depth-first-search)

This is a bit harder than [iterative pre-order](#iterative-pre-order): now we have to check if there is a left or right element or not:
- (START) push current
- if there is left:
  - move left
- else:
  - (ELSE) pop
  - visit
  - if there is right
    - move right
    - GOTO START
  - else:
    - GOTO ELSE

#### Nash equilibrium

↑ **Parent:** [Game theory](#game-theory)  
ⓦ [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash_equilibrium)

The best example to look at first is the [penalty kick left right Nash equilibrium](#penalty-kick-left-right-nash-equilibrium).

Then, a much more interesting example is choosing a deck of a TCG competition: [Magic: The Gathering meta-based deck choice is a bimatrix game](magic-the-gathering.md#magic-the-gathering-meta-based-deck-choice-is-a-bimatrix-game), which is the exact same, but each player has N choices rather than 2.

The next case that should be analyzed is the [prisoner's dilemma](#prisoner-s-dilemma).

The key idea is that:
- imagine that the game will be played many times between two players
- if one player always chooses one deck, the other player will adapt by choosing the anti-deck
- therefore, the best strategy for both players, is to pick decks randomly, each with a certain probability. This type of probabilistic approach is called a [mixed strategy](#mixed-strategy)
- if any player deviates from the equilibrium probability, then the other player can add more of the anti-deck to the deck that the other player deviated, and gain an edge

  Therefore, using equilibrium probabilities is the optimal way to play

##### Penalty kick left right Nash equilibrium

↑ **Parent:** [Nash equilibrium](#nash-equilibrium)

When taking a penalty kick in [soccer](art.md#football), the kicker must chose left or right.

And before he kicks, the goalkeeper must also decide left or right, because there is no time to see where the ball is going.

Because the kicker is right footed however, he kicker kicks better to one side than the other. So we have four probabilities:
- goal kick left keeper jumps left
- goal kick right keeper jumps right
- goal kick left keeper jumps right. Note that it is possible that this won't be a goal, even though the keeper is nowhere near the ball, as the ball might just miss the goal by a bit.
- kick right and keeper jumps left. Analogous to above

### High flying bird vs gophers

↑ **Parent:** [Mathematician](#mathematician)  
🏷️ **Tags:** [Essays by Ciro Santilli](ciro-santilli.md#essays-by-ciro-santilli), [Exploration-exploitation dilemma](machine-learning.md#exploration-exploitation-dilemma)

Ciro once read that there are two types of mathematicians/scientists (he thinks it was comparing Einstein to some Jack of all trades polymath who didn't do any new discoveries):
- high flying birds, who know a bit of everything, feel the beauty of each field, but never dig deep in any of them
- gophers, who dig all the way down, on a single subject, until they either get the [Nobel Prize](nobel-prize.md), or work on the wrong problem and waste their lives

TODO long after Ciro forgot where he had read this from originally, someone later pointed him to: [https://www.ams.org/notices/200902/rtx090200212p.pdf](https://www.ams.org/notices/200902/rtx090200212p.pdf) Birds and Frogs by [Freeman Dyson](physicist.md#freeman-dyson) (2009), which is analogous but about Birds and Frogs. So did [Ciro's memory play a trick on him](ciro-santilli-s-psychology-and-physiology.md#ciro-santilli-s-bad-old-event-memory), or is there also a variant; of this metaphor with a gopher?

[Ciro is without a doubt the bird type](ciro-santilli-s-psychology-and-physiology.md). Perhaps the ultimate scientist is the one who can combine both aspects in the right amount?

Ciro gets bored of things very quickly.

Once he understands the general principles, if the thing is not [the next big thing](ciro-santilli.md#the-next-big-thing), Ciro considers himself satisfied without all the nitty gritty detail, and moves on to the next attempt.

In the field of [mathematics](mathematics.md) for example, Ciro is generally content with understanding cool theorem statements. More generally, one of Ciro's desires is for example to understand the significance of each [physics](physics.md) [Nobel Prize](nobel-prize.md).

This is also very clear for example after Ciro achieved [Linux Kernel Module Cheat](the-most-important-projects-done-by-ciro-santilli.md#linux-kernel-module-cheat): he now had the perfect setup to learn all the Linux kernel shady details but at the same time after all those years he finally felt that "he could do it, so that was enough", and soon moved to other projects.

If Ciro had become a scientist, he would write [the best review papers ever](cirism.md#the-side-effects-of-ambitious-goals-are-often-the-most-valuable-thing-achieved), just like in the current reality he [writes amazing programming tutorials on Stack Overflow](the-most-important-projects-done-by-ciro-santilli.md#ciro-santilli-s-stack-overflow-contributions).

Ciro has in his mind an overly large list of subjects that "he feels he should know the basics of", and whenever he finds something in one of those topics that he does not know enough about, he uncontrollably learns it, even if it is not the most urgent thing to be done. Or at least he puts a mention on his "list of sources" about the subject. Maybe everyone is like that. But Ciro feels that he feels this urge particularly strongly. Correspondingly, if a subject is not in that list, Ciro ignores it without thinking twice.

Ciro believes that high flying birds are the type of people better suited for [venture capital](company.md#venture-capital) investment management: you know a bit of what is hot on several fields to enough depth to decide where to place your bets and how to guide them. But you don't have the patience to actually go deeply into any one of them and deal with each individual [shit](molecular-biology.md#feces) that comes up.

[Cosmos: A Personal Voyage (1980)](science.md#cosmos-a-personal-voyage-1980) episode 1 mentions as quoted by the [Wikipedia page](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Eratosthenes&oldid=1018676910) for [Eratosthenes](science.md#eratosthenes):

> According to an entry in the Suda (a 10th-century encyclopedia), his critics scorned him, calling him [beta](linguistics.md#beta) (the second letter of the [Greek alphabet](linguistics.md#greek-alphabet)) because he always came in second in all his endeavors.

That's Ciro.
